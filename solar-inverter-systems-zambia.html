Solar & Inverter Backup Systems in Zambia: A Buyer's Guide

Long hours of ZESCO load-shedding have turned solar panels and inverters from a nice-to-have into essential kit for Zambian homes and businesses. Unlike a generator, an inverter system switches over silently, needs no fuel and keeps working through the night. But a badly sized or cheaply installed system can leave you in the dark after two hours — so it pays to understand what you are buying.

How an Inverter Backup System Works

An inverter backup system has three core parts: a battery bank that stores energy, an inverter that turns the battery's DC power into the 230V AC power your sockets use, and a charger that refills the batteries — from ZESCO when the grid is on, from solar panels, or both. When load-shedding starts, the inverter detects the loss of supply and takes over in milliseconds, so lights, Wi-Fi, TVs and computers keep running without so much as a flicker.

Add solar panels and a charge controller (built into most modern hybrid inverters) and the system can recharge itself from the sun during the day, cutting your reliance on the grid and your ZESCO bill at the same time.

Inverter-Only, Hybrid or Full Solar?

There are three common set-ups in Zambia, and the right one depends on your budget and how long your outages last:

  • Inverter + batteries (no panels): the cheapest entry point. Batteries charge from ZESCO between outages. Works well when load-shedding blocks are short, but struggles when the grid is off for most of the day and there's no time to recharge.
  • Hybrid solar system: inverter, batteries and solar panels. The sun charges the batteries and powers daytime loads; ZESCO tops up when available. This is the most popular choice for homes, offices and shops because it copes with long outages.
  • Off-grid solar: no grid connection at all — common on farms, lodges and remote sites. It needs a larger battery bank and panel array, and often a generator as a last resort.

Sizing the Inverter

The inverter's rating (in kVA or kW) must cover everything you want running at the same time. Add up the wattage of your essential loads — for example, LED lights (100W), Wi-Fi router (20W), TV (120W), laptop (65W), a fridge (150W running, but a much higher start-up surge) — and choose an inverter with comfortable headroom above that total. Motors in fridges, pumps and air conditioners draw several times their running power for a moment when they start, so a pure sine wave inverter with good surge capacity is a must.

As a rough guide, a typical Lusaka home backing up lights, electronics and a fridge is well served by a 3–5kVA inverter. Small offices and shops often need 5–10kVA, and larger businesses may use three-phase or paralleled units.

Quick tip: Always buy a pure sine wave inverter. Cheaper "modified sine wave" units can make motors run hot and noisy, and may damage sensitive electronics and fridge compressors over time.

Choosing Batteries: Lithium vs Lead-Acid

Batteries are the most expensive part of the system and decide how long your backup lasts. Battery capacity is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh): a 5kWh battery can, in theory, run a 500W load for about 10 hours.

  • Lithium (LiFePO4): higher upfront cost, but lasts 3,000–6,000 cycles, can be discharged deeply (80–90%), charges quickly and needs no maintenance. For daily load-shedding use, lithium usually works out cheaper over the life of the system.
  • Lead-acid / gel / AGM: cheaper to buy, but should only be discharged to about 50% to protect them, last far fewer cycles, and degrade quickly in Zambia's heat. Best for occasional backup rather than daily cycling.

Work out how many hours of backup you need, multiply by the average load, then allow for depth of discharge. If your essentials draw 600W and you want six hours of backup, you need about 3.6kWh of usable energy — roughly a 4–5kWh lithium battery.

How Many Solar Panels Do You Need?

Zambia is blessed with excellent sunshine — around 5 to 6 peak sun hours a day for much of the year. Each 1kW of solar panels produces roughly 4–5kWh on a good day. To recharge a 5kWh battery and run daytime loads, a home typically needs 2–4kW of panels (for example, six to ten 450W panels). Panels should face north, sit at an angle of roughly 15–20 degrees, and be kept clear of shade from trees, water tanks and neighbouring buildings. Remember that output drops in the rainy season, so size with some margin.

What to Connect (and What Not To)

The most cost-effective systems back up essential circuits only: lights, sockets for electronics and Wi-Fi, fridges and freezers, security systems, gate motors and borehole pumps. High-power loads such as geysers, stoves, kettles, irons and large air conditioners drain batteries quickly and push up the inverter size you need. A good installer will split your distribution board into "essential" and "non-essential" circuits so the inverter only ever sees the loads it was designed for. For AC units, consider an energy-efficient inverter model (see our guide to inverter vs non-inverter air conditioners) if you want cooling on backup power.

Installation Matters

Many inverter failures and fires in Zambia come down to poor installation, not poor equipment. A safe installation includes correctly sized DC cables and fuses between batteries and inverter, proper earthing, surge protection on both the AC and solar side, a changeover or bypass switch so the system can be serviced, and a well-ventilated, shaded location for the inverter and batteries. The wiring of your essential circuits should be done by a qualified electrician — our electrical team handles this alongside the inverter install. If you already have a generator, the system can be integrated with an automatic transfer switch for a seamless three-way setup.

Looking After Your System

Solar and inverter systems are low-maintenance, but not no-maintenance. Clean panels every few weeks in the dry season, when dust can cut output by 15–25%. Keep the inverter's vents clear and the battery area cool. Check the inverter display or app for error codes and battery health, and have a technician inspect connections and settings once a year. Frequently Asked Questions

How long will an inverter system run during load-shedding?

It depends on the battery size and the load. A 5kWh lithium battery running 500W of lights, Wi-Fi, TV and a fridge will last roughly 8 to 9 hours. Adding solar panels extends this by recharging the batteries during the day.

Can an inverter run my air conditioner?

Yes, if the inverter and battery bank are sized for it. A small inverter-type AC can run on a 5kVA system, but it will drain batteries much faster than lights and electronics. Most homes back up essentials only and leave large AC units on the grid.

Is solar worth it in Zambia?

With long load-shedding hours and strong year-round sunshine, a hybrid solar system typically pays for itself through avoided generator fuel, fewer spoiled goods and lower ZESCO bills, while providing silent, fuel-free backup.

How long do lithium batteries last?

Quality LiFePO4 batteries are rated for 3,000 to 6,000 cycles. With daily use, that is roughly 8 to 15 years, far longer than lead-acid batteries in Zambia's heat.
